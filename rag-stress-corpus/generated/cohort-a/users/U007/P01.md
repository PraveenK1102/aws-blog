## Where things stand

There is a difference between knowing Redis and defending a decision about Redis under time pressure. Something I keep getting wrong: I describe what I built before explaining why it needed building. My Razorpay round result was passed. Two rounds in, the pattern is clear: I over-prepare for Redis and under-prepare for follow-ups.
## How I prepared

I removed the tool list from my resume and replaced it with three decisions I could defend. For Razorpay, that round ended up rescheduled. The Razorpay process moved faster than I expected, which left less room to recover from a weak answer. I rehearsed the AWS portion so heavily that the unscripted questions caught me flat.

When they asked why PyTorch and not something simpler, I did not have a crisp answer ready. I have started treating each rejection as a list of gaps instead of a verdict on the ML Engineer track.
## The round itself

Practising with a timer changed my answers more than any amount of reading about AWS did. Narrowing to ML Engineer roles felt like giving something up until the replies started improving. My preparation now splits evenly between PostgreSQL fundamentals and talking through the project end to end.
## What I took from it

Reading other people's interview write-ups helps, though their confidence is easy to mistake for my own readiness. The coding round at Razorpay was harder than the practice problems suggested, mainly because I had rehearsed the happy path. One question about AWS exposed that I had memorised the shape of an answer without the reasoning. Did the ML Engineer framing help? Slightly — recruiters asked better questions once I stopped hedging.
