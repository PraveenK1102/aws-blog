## Where things stand

After everything, the outcome at Google was rejected_hiring_manager. Did the Platform / DevOps framing help? Slightly — recruiters asked better questions once I stopped hedging. A recruiter told me the first thirty seconds of a project explanation decide the rest of the conversation. The Google process moved faster than I expected, which left less room to recover from a weak answer. The strongest answer I gave all week was the one where I admitted a trade-off I had got wrong. There is a difference between knowing Java and defending a decision about Java under time pressure. Practising with a timer changed my answers more than any amount of reading about AWS did.
## How I prepared

Two rounds in, the pattern is clear: I over-prepare for Spring and under-prepare for follow-ups. I removed the tool list from my resume and replaced it with three decisions I could defend. I have started treating each rejection as a list of gaps instead of a verdict on the Platform / DevOps track. Something I keep getting wrong: I describe what I built before explaining why it needed building.
## The round itself

One question about Qdrant exposed that I had memorised the shape of an answer without the reasoning. Scheduling practice at the same hour each morning removed most of the friction of starting. ## What I took from it

I stopped applying broadly in March; the reply rate went up almost immediately. Looking back at March, the useful hours were the ones I spent explaining Spring out loud. The take-home at Google was harder than the practice problems suggested, mainly because I had rehearsed the happy path. Rejection stings less when it arrives with a specific reason attached, and this one did.
